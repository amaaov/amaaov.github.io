Second Order Freedom

An ethical test of personal choice through the norms, infrastructure, and refusal costs produced by its repetition.

8 August 2026 · Helsinki · 4 of 4amaaåv

The future is often assembled by people seeking convenience, accessibility, recognition of labour, safety, and connection across distance, with decisions that answer real needs. One person monetises a beloved activity, another introduces a good artist to a larger audience, a third moves a service into an app, and a fourth adds a metric for transparency, and each action can be reasonably defended within one situation.

Repetition changes the boundaries of the situation: an almost free message gives a long silence new social weight, mass adoption of an app-based service allows an organization to reduce its staffed channel to an expensive redundancy, and regular clips from members of a group make the absence of a clip mean the absence of practice because making an action cheaper creates an expectation that eventually closes alternatives.

First-order freedom asks whether I can make my chosen move, while Second Order Freedom looks toward the world that becomes normal when enough people make the same move and institutions rebuild around their choice, bringing a person who may later want to refuse into view.

The phrase has more than one genealogy. Harry Frankfurt distinguished first-order desires from desires about one's own desires, including second-order volitions (Frankfurt 1971); political-economy writing has used second-order choice for choice about the conditions of later choices (Cammack 2014). This essay extends the second line: freedom's object is the shared environment that present decisions leave to the next participant. Personal adjustment of attention already happens inside somebody else’s order of signals, where a person chooses what to look at while the environment has already set the regime in which that choice takes place.

From choice to default

Personal monetisation of a hobby may bring income, recognition, and more time for practice, although its transformation into a sign of seriousness changes the category of amateur activity: unpaid begins to look undervalued, invisible nonexistent, amateur immature, and non-growing unsuccessful. People defending the dignity of creative labour can inadvertently narrow the region where labour was the wrong category.

Publication follows the same path: one clip helps somebody find friends, receive feedback, and preserve a movement, a series can open a career, and the transformation of recording into a membership credential makes the person without a recording explain their belonging. Hobby Crisis studies that change inside a voluntary community, while the mechanism of aggregation matters here because many ordinary choices turned a private path into a social default.

A digital service often begins as a convenient additional channel, after which an organisation observes the majority’s choice, reduces support for other routes, and declares the app its only efficient solution. The compulsory smartphone emerges from a chain of optimisations, each responding to behaviour already altered by earlier decisions.

An emergent norm grows without a single author or finished doctrine through assumptions that feel self-evident: valuable things should become visible, regular activity can be measured, available digitisation should be adopted, and a growing audience is better than a stable small group, with a shared philosophy already acting inside transformations that cease to require discussion.

The price of a cheap signal

A letter required paper, an address, travel, and days of waiting, so silence retained many ordinary explanations. The nearly free instant message created a valuable freedom to sustain relationships across distance, while its mass adoption changed how a pause is interpreted: the person presumably saw the notification, could technically reply, and now owes an explanation for delay. The material cost of a signal approached zero as the social cost of its absence increased.

Publication of amateur work undergoes the same transition because a camera and a channel provide access to learning, archives, and people who could never meet locally, while regularity gradually becomes the background against which an individual silence acquires meaning. Cheap transmission changes the surrounding culture because a possibility used by many becomes an expectation and redistributes the duty to explain oneself.

A network effect intensifies the process: a channel becomes more valuable as more people join it, so every new participant makes their personal choice more reasonable while raising the cost of another route for the next person. An alternative may still exist technically, but an empty chat, a rarely staffed desk, or a forum without replies loses its social function. Once relationships have moved, the organization cuts the fallback budget, workers lose the skill of maintaining it, data accumulates in the new format, and path dependence raises the cost of reversal (Arthur 1989).

Many locally defensible choices can therefore create an outcome nobody ordered separately: a participant chose the convenient channel, a manager reduced duplication, and a developer improved the dominant scenario, while their combined action produced compulsory infrastructure. Second-order freedom attends to this distance between the intention of one decision and the conditions formed after aggregation.

copy 03

Good intention and form

In People with Spark, a form grows from care and later outranks the person, revealing the essential prehistory of Second Order Freedom: pressure can inherit the language of benefit, fairness, and quality. The designer of a ranking wants transparent selection, the festival organiser wants to present strong work, and a participant keeps an archive so memory will endure, while the form receives moral credit from the original intention and keeps spending it after the conditions change.

Work has the same pattern when task visibility aids coordination and gradually turns the visible trace into a substitute for presence and contribution. Past Year Review 24/25 already describes a system reinforcing performed busyness and making people work for the surface of observation; Second Order Freedom adds the design question of which behaviour gains advantage after every participant has learned from this system for several years.

Denmark's digital welfare state gives this pattern a concrete form. Automation and electronic communication were expected to reduce the need for in-person support and improve services, yet Barbara Carreras's ethnography found a different result in the cases studied: access labour moved to individuals, families, and third-sector organizations, while marginalized groups faced greater difficulty obtaining benefits (Carreras 2024). One user's convenience and an agency's saving accumulated into an environment where other people's unpaid labour sustained the promised efficiency.

Moral assessment includes good intention, immediate benefit, and the later path by which a form scales, enters a budget, becomes habit, displaces its fallback, and eventually meets somebody beyond the original design.

copy 04

The repetition test

Before declaring a preference progress, the repetition test traces future conditions of belonging, changes in the cost of refusal, the disappearance of unused skills, channels, and spaces, the distribution of affordable exceptions, and the survival of a return route after organisations invest in the new standard.

The test moves part of the burden from personal choice to its surrounding environment: a career built from a hobby leaves the community responsible for space held by other forms of participation, mandatory recording of every meeting makes people without media traces prove their belonging separately, and developing an app preserves a full route to the service through another channel, which Antidigitism associates with meaningful refusal.

A longitudinal study of the YouTube beauty community shows how entrepreneurial practices adopted by some members altered collective norms and relations for non-entrepreneurial members (Mardon, Cocker, and Daunt 2023); the ordinariness of this bounded case reveals a norm growing through mutual observation, imitation, and environmental adaptation long before a formal requirement appears.

The second order reveals the distribution of benefits and losses: a person earning income from the new norm and a person losing permission to remain an amateur occupy different positions in the same system. A user saving one minute through an app and a person without a compatible device occupy similarly different positions; average convenience hides the edge where refusal becomes expensive.

copy 05

The objection from personal freedom

The repetition test faces an obvious objection: a person who publishes a video, opens a shop, or chooses a convenient app cannot answer for every possible consequence of mass repetition. Requiring every ordinary action to be universalized in advance would immobilize everyday life and burden people with little influence most heavily, while institutions continued setting defaults through budgets, protocols, and access conditions.

Second-order responsibility follows the capacity to alter the shared environment: an individual participant can notice pressure and decline to use their own path as a measure of somebody else’s seriousness; an organizer controls admission rules and must make room for different forms of participation; a platform determines interfaces, portability, and visibility; a state or large organization holds the resources required for equivalent access. The greater the power over a default, the stronger the duty to investigate the system’s edge and finance freedom of refusal.

The repetition test makes consequences visible and helps locate the appropriate level of intervention while preserving room for personal experiment, eccentric projects, and risky new forms. One person may move their whole life into a new channel, while collective responsibility arises when that preference becomes a rule for others. This threshold connects ethics to the distribution of power and avoids imposing the same burden of caution on a user and an institution.

The object worth preserving is a meaningful capability, especially access to an essential service, community, work, and review of a decision. A particular paper form, machine, or obsolete procedure may fall out of use when its function is carried by a genuinely accessible route with different ownership, human review, and acceptable cost. Redundancy consists of several independent ways to exercise a right and allows infrastructure to change without turning technical modernization into a socially closed door.

copy 06

Freedom with reserve

A free environment keeps more possibilities than the majority uses today: an inefficient staffed channel, a venue without compulsory recording, a practice without a career ladder, a pause without explanation, and a place for somebody returning in a changed role, forming a reserve that looks redundant to the current metric and allows a future person to choose differently.

Collective restraint preserves such freedom when the use of an available possibility leaves space around itself, the success of one route preserves the others, a majority preference accounts for the minority, and present convenience leaves autonomy for the future.

In political traditions oriented toward prefiguration, desired relationships begin in the organization of present action (Törnberg 2021): horizontal organization begins by distributing voice inside today’s group, and free infrastructure begins with a route for the dissenting participant before refusal has become widespread. A fallback channel, portable archive, and permission to arrive without a profile look like surplus expenses under current demand, but they give material form to the future ability to choose.

The intergenerational meaning of this reserve comes from the future participant’s inability to consent to today’s default. A budget, archive format, and closed door survive the original authors of a decision, so a preserved alternative lets the next person reconsider their choice in practice even after the established arrangement has become difficult to reverse.

Freedom’s reserve also lives in skills and people a system does not use every minute. A worker able to consider an exception, a local copy, a physical meeting place, and a habit of writing to somebody directly preserve action during failure, conflict, or change of ownership. Such redundancy improves resilience while limiting the primary intermediary’s power because its refusal no longer stops the whole of shared life.

Regular testing keeps a fallback route alive: an occasional request has to reach completion, a local copy has to be restored, a telephone number checked, and an exception reviewed with the person who genuinely holds authority over the decision.

The four articles in this series meet in an environment where a hobby retains permission to remain economically pointless, a digital intermediary remains a tool while exit survives, and a person continues across a pause with the right to end the line; the environment itself holds difference and releases participation from the demand for one persuasive biography.

First-order freedom opens a door before me, while Second Order Freedom leaves another door for the person who arrives later.
