Continuity

Three clocks of human continuity: system records, embodied memory, and the place preserved by relationships.

8 August 2026 · Helsinki · 3 of 4 · amaaåv

The Russian essay Временный чат на годы offers the lived scene of a friendship enduring months and years of silence: its channel helps people find one another after pauses, relational memory carries on without continuous reporting, and three kinds of time move through that bond at once.

A system stores a sequence of registered states, a person continues a practice in body and memory, and other people preserve that person’s place in a relationship; the three forms of continuity support one another while keeping their own rhythms.

time 01

System time

System continuity consists of events that could be registered: publications, logins, versions, metrics, lines in a biography, and account history. A database represents a person as state R₁, then R₂, then R₃, so a year without a new event looks like a break in the sequence.

The strength of system time lies in preservation: a video can wait for twenty years, a name can be indexed, a repository can show the history of a change, a medical record can help a doctor see an old risk, and a registered trace can carry information across distance and human forgetting, while such preservation requires formalisation and keeps the portion of an event that could be translated into the system’s schema. A system can hold those traces for later care or work while leaving the interval between necessary updates free of continuous behavioural logging.

In 2024, I marked twenty years of programming, whose beginning I could place approximately in 2003–2004 because old internet artifacts preserved enough dates and names for verification. The system record gave the biography a confirmed point, although the story itself began before the available trace and consisted for years of activities that had no public address.

I first saw a computer in 1995 at my mother’s workplace and played the DOS version of The Lion King there; several old Macintosh computers appeared later at school, followed by computer clubs and shared-access rooms that sold inexpensive intervals of internet time, magazines, and books, while by 2005 the public networked space already supported lasting relationships across distance. The archive dates the surviving pages accurately and leaves outside them the years of reading, first exercises, and encounters with devices from which this line grew for me.

An archive offers a particular agreement: a system preserves a biography to the extent that the biography accepts its fields, formats, and rules for updates. In a medical history, such formalisation can provide continuity of treatment across doctors; in a repository, it lets somebody continue another person’s work; in a journal, it returns words that memory has long since rewritten. This strength also removes context because the schema confidently stores a registered value while leaving outside the reasons for silence, a changed relationship, and experience for which no field existed.

A preserved record can serve as evidence of the past, while it becomes a source of life through fresh human action. An archive of movement can recall a sequence, although the body has to find balance again; a message history can preserve sentences, although friendship continues through a willingness to answer years later; an old project can compile even though its meaning for the author has ended. The distinction becomes political when the availability of an archive gives an institution or audience grounds for demanding continuation of the earlier role.

A feed adds the rhythm of recency to storage: an old state remains in the archive while a person’s visibility is maintained through new signals, and silence gradually reduces recognisability. “Where are your latest videos?” moves this technical rhythm into conversation and mistakes the absence of a new media object for the absence of a practice. When a representation can circulate without settling what happened, the feed asks for another registered state; Antidigitism names the institutional form of that demand as digitism at the door.

time 02

Personal time

When objects come out after a year in a cupboard, weight returns first, then the familiar placement of the palm, then movement; part of the skill has vanished and part endured without witnesses. Work, illness, another interest, fatigue, or a change in life’s rhythm may have produced the pause while the body continued its relation with the object through recognition, forgetting, and return.

Motor-learning research calls faster reacquisition after prior practice savings, a phenomenon observed across cognitive and movement tasks (Albert et al. 2023). Its mechanisms depend on the task and interval, and rapid relearning does not prove that an intact skill waited in storage. A more modest conclusion is enough here: earlier experience can alter new learning after much of the visible result has decayed.

Personal continuity includes changes that make every return different from restoration: taste moved, the body’s capacities changed, old goals lost their force, and connection with the practice now resides in the ability to join earlier experience with present action.

Machine processing makes this rhythm more visible: in Inferentia & Probabilisticum, an individual computational pass produces an output without a lived thread of consciousness between requests, while the rest of life continues altering a person even during years when the practice receives no attention.

Personal time makes poor evidence because years of an inward relation to music or movement remain invisible, memory fails, skill decays, and desire changes, yet continuity belonging to the person without weekly validation allows a practice to retain meaning during a period when it cannot be presented.

time 03

Relational time

Relational continuity is stored between people: somebody disappears for three years, enters a room, and hears “you are here again” because others held part of their place without a constant presence signal. Familiarity may have weakened and expectations may have changed, but the return meets a shared history contained in no single profile.

Platform life can substitute system time for relational time, turning “we have not seen him for years, but he is one of us” into “he has not posted for years, so he probably stopped.” The substitution appears technical because a feed surfaces recent representations while a room notices a returning body; by adopting the rhythm of a feed, a community delegates part of its responsibility for remembering people to a system.

Relational memory restores belonging while preserving an old role: friends remember the musician, readers await the writer, and a family keeps the image of a talented child. A person returns changed to a place held in an earlier shape, so relational continuity needs permission to revise expectation and release memory from the character of debt.

The right to disappear while remaining part of a relationship separates belonging from permanent availability and exists where a group can say “we have not seen them for a long time” while continuing to regard the bond as real without turning a pause into a disciplinary violation or requiring a declared return date in advance. This tolerance has limits because shared work, care, and promises create concrete obligations; the important distinction lies between an obligation to people and a duty to maintain a continuous machine-readable signal of oneself.

Group memory also takes labour and is distributed unevenly: somebody keeps contacts, invites those who have disappeared, retells the common history to newcomers, and notices when an automated feed has stopped showing a familiar person. Delegating all this work to a platform looks economical until its recency criterion begins deciding whom the group remembers. Relational continuity endures through particular gestures, including an occasional letter, an open invitation, and recognition after a long silence.

time 04

The right to break

A modern public biography favours a continuous line: interest becomes skill, skill becomes output, output becomes identity, and identity begins to demand continued development. A stop quickly receives a story about a lost audience, unrealised talent, and departure from the path. An archive exposes the earlier role to other people and raises the price of the next step.

Developmental psychology, including James Marcia’s work, describes identity through exploration and commitment, offering a cautious analogy for a temporary interest: it creates space for trying a possible self, while early public fixation adds witnesses to the commitment. Within those limits, the framework shows how “this could become a career” opens a path while moving the value of the present into an imagined future.

Hobby Crisis already examines early audiences through Pérez-Torres’s review and the cost of stopping. The next step here belongs to the right to break: the ability to quit protects exploration because identity formation needs room to reconsider earlier commitments.

Continuity remains human while it includes the right to end a line: a person may leave without a farewell post, decline to explain an absence, return without a comeback narrative, rename the activity, or finish forever. The right to quit preserves the reality of the hour already lived, which remains part of a biography regardless of the later decision.

The same right protects a long period of weak skill in which awkward music delights the player, a needless program solves one domestic problem, and a drawing completes its whole purpose while the hand learns to see. Mastery sometimes grows from this region, whose value has already occurred in the time lived there.

time 05

The right to participate

In a voluntary practice, quality does not determine a person’s right to continue. A person may play the same three chords for years, repeat one comfortable trick, assemble a technically absurd device, or draw without visible progress because the activity shapes an evening, gives the body a rhythm, sustains friendship, or remains a pleasant way to think. Evaluation remains available and sometimes welcome, while it receives no jurisdiction over participation itself.

Optimization changes this agreement when every session has to improve a measurable indicator and justify the next. A plateau becomes a problem, a change of criteria looks like lost focus, and return at an easier level reads as regression, although human practice moves through injury, age, new curiosity, and varying amounts of free time. Continuity then requires permission to proceed with less skill than before and permission to decide again what counts as good.

External reward can sustain learning, but an early attachment of practice to recognition changes the answer to why a person returns. Work by Lepper and colleagues, and by Ryan and Deci, shows that the effect depends on the design and context of a reward, suggesting a need for institutional care: a venue, school, and family can mark achievement while preserving a full place for play that proves nothing.

time 06

Pause, care, and return

A pause can be freely chosen or caused by depression, illness, poverty, care for somebody else, loss of housing, migration, or lost access to a place and its objects. Romanticising an interval can conceal these causes and leave the person without help, so the right to pause includes care for the conditions of return: an accessible hall, a kept instrument, a message without a demand for explanation, a flexible fee, and memory that an absence may have been forced.

A musician who loses housing may sell an instrument and stop answering the group while an archive continues displaying concerts and an earlier professional role. Two years later, help with an instrument and rehearsal space creates a real possibility of return, but skill, hearing, and desire have changed, while the person may chiefly need continued connection with friends. Care in this situation supports several outcomes at once: playing together at a new level, attending without performing, taking another role around the group, or quietly completing the musical part of their shared history.

Care leaves the person free to continue, finish, move to another practice, or keep the earlier experience without a new commitment; when support requires a return, it becomes a method of restoring old productivity and brings the same debt back under a benevolent name. Respect for continuity keeps a route available while leaving its direction to the person whose life has changed.

Another person also returns: the body carries injury and age, social roles change, an old technique loses its attraction, and the earlier group has developed a new language. A real return creates a relationship between past experience and present capacities. Sometimes it produces a new form of practice, while sometimes one meeting completes the old line, and in both cases archive, body, and relationships can acknowledge what was lived without demanding endless continuation.

time 07

Three clocks

The conflict among three times can appear in a single return, when a profile shows a last publication from five years ago and classifies the person as inactive, the body recognizes a familiar movement together with new limits, and friends remember earlier skill while meeting the participant of today. An error begins whenever one clock receives authority over the others: an archive demands conformity with a saved image, embodied memory persuades the person that an earlier group role still applies, or somebody else’s memory refuses to let them finish.

A more careful agreement uses each source in its proper domain: the system preserves available evidence and shows its gaps honestly, the body determines present capacity, relationships offer connection without guaranteeing the old form, and a decision about continuation emerges among them in present time. Continuity then becomes a way of joining changes while preserving the right to an irreversible break.

Keeping the clocks distinct also makes conflict visible in time: a technical task calls for restoration of a record, care for the body calls for a slower pace, and relationships can keep an invitation open while the person decides what form of continuation is currently available.

The three clocks stay distinct while a silent interval holds forgetting, reconsideration, another person’s care, and ordinary life. A hand may eventually lift the object from the floor, or the cupboard may stay closed; both outcomes belong to the biography that was lived.
