On fixed gear, pedal resistance holds both speed and a feel for where the wheel is. On freewheel you stop pedaling and the wheel keeps turning, while speed stays in the brake lever and in how you sit over the seat. Backward tips come when the wheel runs out and there is no pedal to push against; forward tips come when a locked drive no longer scrubs the roll. A solid fixed rider still hits asphalt the first week if they expect “same unicycle, loose hub.”

The path is short: real fixed-gear control first, then gliding and coasting in the body before the free hub, a quiet back, shoulders forward through braking, brake and coast as one skill. These are one rider’s notes from community routes and lived falls; they sit beside local coaching while the skill is new.

**Risk.** Freewheel riding can cause serious injury. Wear a helmet and pads, start on soft ground away from traffic, and stop when tired or hurt. Local coaching and supervised practice beat following text alone when the skill is new.

## Before you touch a freewheel

Solid fixed-gear riding comes first: mount and dismount without drama, then ride flat, gentle hills, and rough ground until looking around and changing speed is boring. If every outing on fixed is still an adventure, freewheel will only multiply that adventure into hospital paperwork.

Hand-brake time on a fixed unicycle helps a lot. Many muni and road setups already have a disc; use it until feathering the lever while pedaling feels ordinary, because that habit carries straight onto the free hub.

Gliding and coasting on fixed gear are strong warm-ups for the freewheel in this route, even if you never get pretty at them. Other riders enter through mounting and pedaling first; either path still needs empty-pedal balance before long free coasts. Gliding with feet on the frame or pegs, wheel rolling and body balancing, teaches the seat–hip relationship without crank timing. Coasting with the brake, feet off the pedals while you slow and balance on the lever, is the closest rehearsal of freewheel’s empty pedals; soft downhill on grass or smooth dirt is enough. Corbin Dunn’s brake-assisted coasting and David Weichenberger’s long coasts are useful mirrors if you give the body hours of wheel moving with feet idle.

Give the body those two feelings first, and the freewheel’s first coast shrinks to “same empty pedals, new clutch noise.”

Wear a BMX helmet, knee and elbow pads, and gloves, and start on grass or soft dirt clear of traffic, dogs, and street furniture. Skip sessions when tired, drunk, or already hurt: freewheel falls punish impatience harder than fixed falls do.

## Posture: shoulders forward, butt for the back seat

A large share of early freewheel falls come from trying to steer balance with the upper body the way a nervous fixed rider does when tired. The back rounds, the shoulders drop back, the head folds, and the hips arrive late; on freewheel the cranks no longer carry wheel resistance, so that late hip plus a shoulder-back lean is often the whole story of the crash.

Keep the back straight and the torso quiet, chest open and eyes out, with forward lean meaning the shoulders commit forward. Saving a tip by throwing the shoulders farther into a fall only accelerates the tip; pulling the shoulders back while you brake is worse, a heavy uncontrollable dump with high injury risk.

On freewheel, any lean backward lives only in the butt and the seat. Tip the pelvis a hair back through the saddle when the wheel wants to run out ahead of you; keep the shoulders forward through that seat shift and through the brake. Fixed gear still lets many riders lean the shoulders back heavily and catch the balance with the feet on locked cranks; freewheel removes that foot catch, so the same shoulder-back habit into the lever lands hard.

If you catch yourself hunching to look at the tire or the brake lever, stop, remount with a wall or tree, and reset the tall seat with shoulders still forward. Looking down invites the rounded back that dumps people backward when the free hub spins and the torso was already folded.

Practice the split on fixed gear first (shoulders forward, seat adjustments through the butt) during ordinary rides and glides; freewheel only amplifies whatever sitting habit you bring.

## Brakes: the only reliable no

On freewheel the brake is the main way to refuse speed. Pedals drive forward when engaged and leave you free when you idle, so learn the lever as a continuous instrument.

Learning the brake means learning the forward lean at the same time. In early sessions, set the shoulders forward before the first pulse and keep them there through each stop: start with the brake held and that lean already set, release into motion with it, and keep the shoulders forward while you feather and stop. A seat shift back through the butt is fine when the wheel runs ahead; shoulders stay forward through that shift and through the scrub. Focus the first brake sessions on shoulders-forward scrubbing; the lever hand follows that posture.

The early mount is static with the brake on: set one pedal down and the other up, put the mounting foot on the down pedal, hold the lever so the wheel stays still, then sit. Hold the brake firmly when mounting and when sitting still against a wall, and release into motion only when the shoulders and hips have already tipped slightly forward and the first pedal stroke is ready. Releasing while still sitting upright, or with the shoulders already back, hoping the pedals will catch is a classic backward dump. Rolling mounts and jump mounts belong in continuous riding, once the static start is boring.

While riding, feather with light pulses that teach how much lever moves the balance point, shoulders still committed forward. Hard grabs from high speed pitch you forward if the hips stay back, or lock you into a stop–wobble if you were already soft in the core, so prefer slowing early over heroic last-second stops.

Early on, heavy brake use is fine. Keep the lever in the ride while the shoulders-forward habit settles; relax the grip with progression, and only later leave longer stretches of brakeless coasting and pedaling. Alternate a stretch of pulsed braking with the shoulders-forward lean held, then lengthen the quiet-lever coasts between pulses, then a full stop without stepping off, aiming for a rider who can scrub and float in the same outing.

If you are going over backward and the lever will not save you, many riders throw the unicycle clear and step off so they are not tangled with a spinning freewheel and a brake hose; practice that exit only where the landing is safe. Forward falls want the same early honesty: step off before the pitch rides you into the ground with locked arms.

Slight downhill helps the first starts; extreme downhill comes after feathering and posture are boring on the flat.

## Two doors into the same room

Some riders enter with the brake always available (finnspin’s tutorial path), which lowers fear and often means more lever time at first. That overuse is allowed, and the hand relaxes as balance grows into longer coast and pedal stretches. Others enter without relying on the brake early (AJ Kinsella-Johnson’s mounting/pedaling then coasting pair), which builds float sooner and bruises more. Either door works if the other skill is scheduled on purpose, because coasting and braking are one curriculum with two chapters.

Carl Hunt’s short and longer freewheel tutorials (shared on the Unicyclist megathread), UniMyra’s riding series, and that megathread remain the living scrapbook. Simon Jan du Voyage’s street rides and indoor freewheel training are dense inspiration once coast and brake share a line; Rebekka Wiedener’s Freewheel Revolution and Tomas Haluza’s FlickFlock footage sit with the hub half of the same album. Watch for posture as much as for tricks: shoulders forward, butt handling the back seat, lever hand calm.

## Exercise ladder

Distances are targets for when an exercise starts to feel usable.

### 1. Sitting and feet (0 m)

At a wall or tree, set one pedal down and the other up, put the mounting leg on the down pedal, hold the brake so the wheel cannot roll, then mount and sit tall with shoulders forward and weight into the seat until you can find the up pedal and the brake hand without looking down. Ten quiet minutes beats ten nervous seconds; the goal is a stable sit you could photograph, which is a setup drill before the quest grades begin.

### 2. Coast without the lever (10–50 m)

Mount as above, then open the arms with hands off seat and brake, tip from the hips into a soft roll, and stay tall. When balance leaves, step off and let the uni go, stretching the coast as gliding’s cousin with pedals under empty feet. Early on, use assisted acceleration: a wall push, a partner shove, or a short run-up that loads speed before the free coast.

### 3. Start and stop with the brake (10–50 m)

Use a slight downhill if needed, set the shoulders forward before you touch the lever for motion, release into the first revolution with that lean held, take a few strokes like fixed gear, then make a controlled stop with the shoulders still forward, and finish with a clean dismount. Shoulders back into the brake dumps you hard and often out of control; keep the shoulders forward through the stop, and use only the butt for any backward seat shift. If the backward tip starts, throw the uni clear, and prefer early small stops over late violent ones.

### 4. Brake and coast in motion (50–100 m)

Use the same shoulders-forward start, pulse the brake every half-second for a stretch with that lean kept (early sessions may keep the lever busy, and that is fine), then lengthen coasts between pulses as the hand relaxes, pedaling only enough to keep the line, since extra cadence makes stops sharper and falls harder to control. Practice full stops without dismounting, find balance again, and continue, keeping the shoulders out of the steering and still forward into each scrub. While rolling forward, pedal the cranks backward under free feet without engaging the drive, and hold different crank positions (horizontal, near-vertical, mid-angle) for a few metres each.

### 5. Continuous riding, mounts, terrain (100 m+)

Ride a loop so failures stay on the same path. Keep using the static brake-on mount (down pedal, up pedal) until riding itself is boring, then add stand-jump and rolling-jump mounts, later with less brake. Add gentle up and down and pumptrack when control is honest; practice small hops and firm stops that stay mounted. Practice a crank flip while braking (reposition ~180° under the lever). On a gentle climb, let the uni fade into a roll-back, then restore forward pedaling; much later, try a short uphill backward attempt that returns to forward travel. Extreme downhill and showy stops come after posture and feathering no longer need conscious narration.

## Freewheel quest / grades

There is no official freewheel skill ladder. On the older Official IUF Skill Levels cards, Level 8 asks for a *glide* and Level 10 for a *coast* on fixed gear. The separate Freestyle Levels page places gliding and coasting differently, and those cards cover fixed-gear freestyle only. The grades below are a working quest for this path: cumulative, mixing brake and coast, with posture as a pass condition. Still sitting with the brake locked is a warm-up drill; grades begin when the freewheel is in motion. Full interactive checklist: [Free-Wheel Unicycle Skill Quest](20260804130000_free_wheel_unicycle_skill_quest.html).

**Entry gate.** On fixed gear, show solid riding until looking around is boring, brake feathering while pedaling, an honest gliding attempt, and an honest brake-assisted coast. On freewheel, complete the supported static mount described above, settle without looking down, and dismount cleanly; return to fixed gear if the fixed gate is not yet reliable.

| Grade | Name | What you show |
|-------|------|----------------|
| **FW1 Start** | First ride | Static brake-on mount (down/up pedals); shoulders-forward lean into the first revolution; ride 25–50 m tall; controlled stop with shoulders still forward; coast ≥10 m hands off the lever; assisted push or run-up into a free coast; practiced clear throw-exit if the backward tip wins. |
| **FW2 Brake–coast** | Mix | Same static brake-on mount unassisted on flat; pulse brake with shoulders forward then coast ≥20 m; full stop without dismounting, regain balance, continue (≥3 linked cycles); ≥100 m continuous with pedal and coast; tall posture and shoulders-forward scrub through stop; pedal cranks backward while rolling forward; hold at least three crank clock positions while coasting. |
| **FW3 Continuous** | Outing | ≥500 m with real coast segments; first stand-jump or rolling-jump mount (after the static mount is boring); gentle up and down with early feathering; firm stop from moderate speed, stay mounted; crank flip ~180° while braking; uphill fade into roll-back then restore forward. |
| **FW4 Terrain & options** | Ground | Undulating path or pumptrack-style transitions; intentional small hop; coast ≥50 m in one float; remount after a chosen tip. Electives (log at least one): extreme stop stay mounted; trail freewheel with a declared brake plan; uphill backward attempt then restore forward. |

Self- or peer-attestation means filming the required list, naming wheel size, hub, brake, and surface, and noting whether the back rounded, the lever arrived after the lower pedal, or the shoulders retreated during braking. Grades mark completed tasks.

The exercise ladder feeds the quest in order, from sitting as the setup drill through free coast and start/stop into FW1, pulse and coast in motion into FW2, then continuous mounts and terrain into FW3–FW4. The feeling called “learned” below is roughly solid FW2 with FW3 underway.

## What “learned” feels like

You can start on flat without a wall, ride a few hundred meters while alternating coast and pedal, stop fully without stepping off, and remount without panic. The torso stays long when the surface gets interesting, the brake hand is boring, and falls still happen, though they are shorter and more chosen. On the quest sheet that is FW2 held and FW3 in progress.

Then the megathread’s other half begins with hub choices, crank length (often a size shorter than fixed), disc setup, Flik-Flok versus Pipifax, and trail noise. Hardware comes after the sitting and the lever are already true, and this page stays with the body.
