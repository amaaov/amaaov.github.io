On fixed gear, pedal resistance holds both speed and a feel for where the wheel is. On freewheel you stop pedaling and the wheel keeps turning, while speed stays in the brake lever and in how you sit over the seat. Backward tips come when the wheel runs out and there is no pedal to push against; forward tips come when a locked drive no longer scrubs the roll. A solid fixed rider still hits asphalt the first week if they expect “same unicycle, loose hub.”

The path is short: real fixed-gear control first, then gliding and coasting in the body before the free hub, a long torso with seat-shift through braking, brake and coast as one skill. These are one rider’s notes from community routes and lived falls; they sit beside local coaching while the skill is new.

**Risk.** Freewheel riding can cause serious injury. Wear a helmet and pads, start on soft ground away from traffic, and stop when tired or hurt. Local coaching and supervised practice beat following text alone when the skill is new.

## Before you touch a freewheel

Hand-brake time on a fixed unicycle helps a lot. Many muni and road setups already have a disc; use it until feathering the lever while pedaling feels ordinary, because that habit carries straight onto the free hub.

Gliding and coasting on fixed gear are strong warm-ups for the freewheel in this route, even if you never get pretty at them. Other riders enter through mounting and pedaling first; either path still needs empty-pedal balance before long free coasts. Gliding with feet on the frame or pegs, wheel rolling and body balancing, teaches the seat–hip relationship without crank timing. Coasting with the brake, feet off the pedals while you slow and balance on the lever, is the closest rehearsal of freewheel’s empty pedals; soft downhill on grass or smooth dirt is enough. Corbin Dunn’s brake-assisted coasting and David Weichenberger’s long coasts are useful mirrors if you give the body hours of wheel moving with feet idle.

Give the body those two feelings first, and the freewheel’s first coast shrinks to “same empty pedals, new clutch noise.”

Wear a BMX helmet, knee and elbow pads, and gloves, and start on grass or soft dirt clear of traffic, dogs, and street furniture. Skip sessions when tired, drunk, or already hurt: freewheel falls punish impatience harder than fixed falls do.

## Posture: long torso, butt for the back seat

Early freewheel falls often come from trying to steer balance with the upper body the way a nervous fixed rider does when tired. The back rounds, the shoulders collapse back, the head folds, and the hips arrive late; on freewheel the cranks no longer carry wheel resistance, so mass often ends up behind the contact patch while the hips are still late, and gravity accelerates the backward tip. The rounded collapse is how that aft mass often arrives, not the cause by itself.

After long confident fixed-gear riding, the unfamiliar drill is committing mass forward into coasts, starts, and brake work—sometimes boldly. The upright sit that felt safe on fixed leaves mass behind the contact patch on freewheel, and gravity then accelerates a hard backward dump. Forward commitment over the wheel is what freewheel asks for, and it feels wrong until the body owns it.

Keep the back straight and the torso quiet, chest open and eyes out. Into a coast, the first pedal stroke, or a brake pulse, lean means the shoulders commit over the wheel. Looking down into a rounded collapse often leaves mass behind the contact patch, and gravity dumps the tip hard when the free hub spins; that aft-mass dump is different from the deliberate rearward seat shift braking needs.

On freewheel the split is this: shift the pelvis back through the saddle under scrub, and keep the torso long with mass still committed forward over the wheel. Tip the pelvis farther back as the lever hardens, because deceleration throws the body forward relative to the contact patch, and the seat shift answers that without giving up the forward lean. Fixed gear still lets many riders lean the shoulders back heavily and catch the balance with the feet on locked cranks; freewheel removes that foot catch, so the old upright or shoulder-back habit parks mass behind the contact patch and gravity finishes the dump.

If you catch yourself hunching to look at the tire or the brake lever, stop, remount with a wall or tree, and reset the tall forward sit. Looking down invites a fold that leaves mass aft of the wheel; when the free hub spins, gravity accelerates that backward tip.

Practice the split on fixed gear first (long torso, seat adjustments through the butt, deliberate forward tip) during ordinary rides and glides; freewheel only amplifies whatever sitting habit you bring, and it demands a forward commitment fixed gear rarely required at the same intensity.

## Brakes: the lever that refuses speed

On freewheel the brake is the main way to refuse speed along the brake-first path. Pedals drive forward when engaged and leave you free when you idle, so learn the lever as a continuous instrument.

Learning the brake means learning the seat shift under scrub and the forward commitment that keeps mass from falling aft. In early sessions, start with a tall open torso already tipped forward—sometimes more forward than feels polite after fixed—then shift the pelvis farther back as each pulse hardens: hold the brake with that tall forward sit already set, release into the first revolution, and feather while you match the pelvis to the scrub. Focus the first brake sessions on scrubbing with honest forward lean plus an honest seat shift; the lever hand follows that posture.

The early mount is static with the brake on: set one pedal down and the other up, put the mounting foot on the down pedal, hold the lever so the wheel stays still, then sit. Hold the brake firmly when mounting and when sitting still against a wall, and release into motion only when the shoulders and hips have already tipped slightly forward and the first pedal stroke is ready. Releasing while still sitting upright, or already collapsed back through the shoulders, hoping the pedals will catch is a classic backward dump. Rolling mounts and jump mounts belong in continuous riding, once the static start is boring.

While riding, feather with light pulses that teach how much lever moves the balance point, torso still long. Hard grabs from high speed pitch you forward if the hips stay ahead of the scrub, or lock you into a stop–wobble if you were already soft in the core, so prefer slowing early over heroic last-second stops.

Early on, heavy brake use is fine. Keep the lever in the ride while the tall-seat habit settles; relax the grip with progression, and only later leave longer stretches of brakeless coasting and pedaling. Alternate a stretch of pulsed braking with the seat answering each pulse, then lengthen the quiet-lever coasts between pulses, then a full stop without stepping off, aiming for a rider who can scrub and float in the same outing.

If you are going over backward and the lever will not save you, many riders throw the unicycle clear and step off so they are not tangled with a spinning freewheel and a brake hose; practice that exit only where the landing is safe. Forward falls want the same early honesty: step off before the pitch rides you into the ground with locked arms.

Slight downhill helps the first starts; extreme downhill comes after feathering and posture are boring on the flat.

## Two doors into the same room

Some riders enter with the brake always available (finnspin’s tutorial path), which lowers fear and often means more lever time at first. That overuse is allowed, and the hand relaxes as balance grows into longer coast and pedal stretches. Others enter without relying on the brake early (AJ Kinsella-Johnson’s mounting/pedaling then coasting pair), which builds float sooner and bruises more. Either door works if the other skill is scheduled on purpose, because coasting and braking are one curriculum with two chapters.

Carl Hunt’s short and longer freewheel tutorials (shared on the Unicyclist megathread), UniMyra’s riding series, and that megathread remain the living scrapbook. Simon Jan du Voyage’s street rides and indoor freewheel training are dense inspiration once coast and brake share a line; Rebekka Wiedener’s Freewheel Revolution and Tomas Haluza’s Flick Flock footage sit with the hub half of the same album. Watch for posture as much as for tricks: long torso, butt handling the back seat, lever hand calm.

## Exercise ladder

Distances are targets for when an exercise starts to feel usable.

### 1. Sitting and feet (0 m)

At a wall or tree, set one pedal down and the other up, put the mounting leg on the down pedal, hold the brake so the wheel cannot roll, then mount and sit tall with shoulders forward and weight into the seat until you can find the up pedal and the brake hand without looking down. Ten quiet minutes beats ten nervous seconds; the goal is a stable sit you could photograph, which is a setup drill before the quest grades begin.

### 2. Coast without the lever (10–50 m)

Mount as above, then open the arms with hands off seat and brake, tip from the hips into a soft roll, and stay tall. When balance leaves, step off and let the uni go, stretching the coast as gliding’s cousin with pedals under empty feet. Early on, use assisted acceleration: a wall push or partner support that loads speed before the free coast.

### 3. Start and stop with the brake (10–50 m)

Use a slight downhill if needed, set a tall open torso with a slight forward tip before you touch the lever for motion, release into the first revolution with that tip held, take a few strokes like fixed gear, then make a controlled stop with the torso still long and the butt taking the rearward seat shift the scrub asks for, and finish with a clean dismount. If mass ends up behind the contact patch—often after collapsing into the lever—gravity accelerates a heavy, hard-to-control dump; keep mass managed through the stop with a long torso. If the backward tip starts, throw the uni clear, and prefer early small stops over late violent ones.

### 4. Brake and coast in motion (50–100 m)

Use the same tall start, pulse the brake every half-second for a stretch while the seat answers each pulse (early sessions may keep the lever busy, and that is fine), then lengthen coasts between pulses as the hand relaxes, pedaling only enough to keep the line, since extra cadence makes stops sharper and falls harder to control. Practice full stops without dismounting, find balance again, and continue, keeping the shoulders out of the steering and the torso long into each scrub. While rolling forward, pedal the cranks backward under free feet without engaging the drive, and hold different crank positions (horizontal, near-vertical, mid-angle) for a few metres each.

### 5. Continuous riding, mounts, terrain (100 m+)

Ride a loop so failures stay on the same path. Keep using the static brake-on mount (down pedal, up pedal) until riding itself is boring, then add stand-jump and rolling-jump mounts, later with less brake. Add gentle up and down and pumptrack when you already ride steadily; practice small hops and firm stops that stay mounted. Practice a crank-flip with the brake held: roll the pedals backward only (feet on or off) into a new position. With the brake on, the flip only goes backward. On a gentle climb, let the uni fade into a roll-back, then restore forward pedaling—freewheel has no pedal drive for controlled reverse travel. Extreme downhill and extreme stops that stay mounted come after posture and feathering no longer need conscious narration. Once free coast is already solid, an optional advanced gag is a soft-rope tow-coast behind a bicycle or another unicycle: the rope passes light pull while you float.

## Freewheel quest / grades

There is no official freewheel skill ladder. On the older Official IUF Skill Levels cards, Level 8 asks for a *glide* and Level 10 for a *coast* on fixed gear. The separate Freestyle Levels page places gliding and coasting differently, and those cards cover fixed-gear freestyle only. The grades below are a working quest for this path: cumulative, mixing brake and coast, with posture as a pass condition. Still sitting with the brake locked is a warm-up drill; grades begin when the freewheel is in motion. Full interactive checklist: [Free-Wheel Unicycle Skill Quest](20260804130000_free_wheel_unicycle_skill_quest.html).

**Entry gate.** On fixed gear, show solid riding until looking around is boring, brake feathering while pedaling, an honest gliding attempt, and an honest brake-assisted coast. On freewheel, complete the supported static mount described above, settle without looking down, and dismount cleanly; return to fixed gear if the fixed gate is not yet reliable.

| Grade | Name | What you show |
|-------|------|----------------|
| **FW1 Start** | First ride | Static brake-on mount (down/up pedals); tall open torso with a slight forward tip into the first revolution; ride 25–50 m tall; controlled stop with long torso and seat-shift through the scrub; coast ≥10 m hands off the lever; wall push or partner support into a free coast; practiced clear throw-exit if the backward tip wins. |
| **FW2 Brake–coast** | Mix | Same static brake-on mount unassisted on flat; pulse brake with the seat answering then coast ≥20 m; full stop without dismounting, regain balance, continue (≥3 linked cycles); ≥100 m continuous with pedal and coast; tall posture and long-torso scrub through stop; pedal cranks backward while rolling forward; hold at least three crank clock positions while coasting. |
| **FW3 Continuous** | Outing | ≥500 m with real coast segments; first stand-jump or rolling-jump mount (after the static mount is boring); gentle up and down with early feathering; firm stop from moderate speed, stay mounted; crank-flip with brake held (pedals backward only, feet on or off); uphill fade into roll-back then restore forward. |
| **FW4 Terrain & options** | Ground | Undulating path or pumptrack-style transitions; intentional small hop; coast ≥50 m in one float; remount after a chosen tip. Electives (log at least one): extreme stop stay mounted; trail freewheel with a declared brake plan; soft-rope tow-coast once free coast is solid (advanced gag). |

Self- or peer-attestation means filming the required list, naming wheel size, hub, brake, and surface, and noting whether mass sat behind the contact patch, the lever arrived after the lower pedal, or a collapse left mass aft during braking. Grades mark completed tasks.

The exercise ladder feeds the quest in order, from sitting as the setup drill through free coast and start/stop into FW1, pulse and coast in motion into FW2, then continuous mounts and terrain into FW3–FW4. The feeling called “learned” below is roughly solid FW2 with FW3 underway.

## What “learned” feels like

You can start on flat without a wall, ride a few hundred meters while alternating coast and pedal, stop fully without stepping off, and remount without panic. The torso stays long on bumps and turns, the hand reaches for the brake less often, and falls still happen, though they are shorter and more chosen. On the quest sheet that is FW2 held and FW3 in progress.
