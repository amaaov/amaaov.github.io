The digital within the living

amaaov

12 November 2026

Read the essay
+

I have seen people cover soil with asphalt and put buildings where trees could have grown. Afterwards, the space has a purpose that is easy to recognise. Somebody can walk across it, drive over it, enter through a door. I understand the convenience, yet I keep thinking about the ground underneath and the possibilities we have closed there. I wonder how much room is left for anything to grow there.

The same thought follows me when dense bushes, fallen trees and loose organic material are taken away. A place becomes easier to pass through and inspect. Whatever remains has a more definite place in the city, while the wildness that made it difficult to read has diminished. I recognise something of my own daily work in the satisfaction of this change. I clean up software, and I want the result to be clear, usable and easier for someone else to maintain. I am used to treating that satisfaction as a sign of care.

At work, I can usually give an account of what the cleanup is for. Somewhere there is a demand on another person's attention, a task that keeps running, a promise we can no longer keep. I want to follow it far enough to know what can end. Outdoors, the wish to finish can arrive before I have understood what continues in the material I am looking at. A branch on the ground seems to be waiting for someone to do something with it. It takes an effort to let that first impression remain undecided.

Under fallen leaves, there may already be insects passing through the winter. The litter helps shape the moisture and temperature around them; removing it can affect what emerges later. Those consequences are harder to see than the cleared ground. I can look at a tidy place now and feel that the work has been done well, while some of what has changed will become apparent only in another season. By then, the absence may be difficult to connect with the moment that produced it.

A fallen tree makes that continuation easier for me to imagine. Its wood changes as it decays, and different fungi can find suitable conditions at different stages. What looks like a single object being lost can contain many lives whose circumstances are changing. Carrying the wood away takes those circumstances away with it. Whatever can return afterwards will meet changed conditions, even if the ground soon looks green again.

I keep calling it a fallen tree because I can recognise what it used to be. For the organisms using it, the wood has a present that this description scarcely reaches. Their lives proceed through changes in the material, at speeds that need not suit our plans for the place. In a waterlogged peatland, plant remains can accumulate because decomposition is slow; drainage changes the conditions that allow this to happen. I have to make room for both kinds of time, for the wood changing and the peat accumulating. My wish to see an area finished tells me little about what either place needs.

This is where the resemblance to my work begins to trouble me. Software also has dependencies I may fail to notice. Somebody may still use a small service; a record may preserve evidence that cannot be reconstructed. I can remove an apparent leftover and discover that another person had made a place for it in their day. I owe that possibility attention. Yet a forgotten task can also go on consuming resources after anybody has stopped wanting its result, and an abandoned interface can continue to invite reliance. Leaving everything running would make other people carry the cost of my reluctance to decide.

I want to be able to end that obligation without erasing everything that happened. Code can remain available after active maintenance ends. An archived repository can still be searched and forked; a description of a method can survive the service that once carried it out. These choices ask different things of the people involved. Keeping a contribution available need not mean keeping its author permanently at work. I would like the decision to preserve something to leave room for a person to stop.

I can let a program stop and still need the living world that made my work possible. People build the machines, agree on what their operations mean and organise their continued use. We do this as living beings who need food, water, air and somewhere to live. I understand the digital world as our social creation within those conditions. A file may outlast its author, while the practices of reading and using it still depend on people. For me, the living world matters more, because even the people who give our digital purposes meaning depend on it.

The machine still has a physical cost while any of this continues. Electricity, equipment and the manufacture of that equipment belong in the account. Processor time, memory, network traffic and storage help me examine the demand, though a count of deleted bytes cannot tell me by itself how much electricity has been saved. I need to know what changes in the actual equipment and workload. The consequences of software extend into the material world even when I meet them first as a maintenance task on a screen.

They can reach living organisms through equipment too. A computer can regulate the conditions of a culture, using feedback and light to change gene expression and cell growth. People have built such arrangements, and each adjustment can happen automatically after the system has been set up. The cells still need their medium and the apparatus still needs energy and care. Switching off such a system could alter the conditions on which the culture depends.

When I describe the computer's effect on that culture, I can include the lamp and feedback equipment in what I call the system. At that scale, the system regulates growth directly. If I follow the mechanism, I find a sequence of computation, electrical control, light and a cellular response. Leaving the intermediate steps out of a description changes the scale at which I speak; the steps still have to occur. This is how something made within the living world can come to act back upon it. I need to follow those connections when deciding what can be switched off, just as I need to notice what inhabits a log before taking it away.

Information leaves room for a future that I care about. Somebody may find a description of a species, recognise what is happening nearby and decide to leave a patch of ground alone. Between finding the text and changing the place lie attention, judgment and the means to act. The information can help make the change possible, while the organism will live with whatever conditions finally exist there. I value the text for this possibility, and I want to keep sight of the distance it still has to travel.

The organisms in the fallen wood can continue without anybody taking that journey on their behalf. A fungus does not need us to recognise its use for a log before it begins to grow. This independence is part of what I mean by wildness. I want some life in the city to have room beyond the purposes we have already assigned. That asks me to tolerate a place whose value I cannot fully explain, even while I can easily explain how to make it neater.

The difficulty follows me into the word garbage. Even floating plastic can become a place where coastal organisms live and reproduce, as researchers have documented in the North Pacific. I cannot make those lives disappear from the decision by naming the material pollution. I also cannot infer from their presence that leaving the plastic is good: the same rafts can carry organisms to other places, and the harms remain to be considered. Once something is inhabited, removing it has another consequence. It still takes judgment to decide what to do.

A city needs paths people can use and places where they can be safe. Sometimes a fallen tree has to move. Elsewhere, leaving wood or changing what happens around it may let people and other organisms continue to use the area. Care becomes more demanding as I notice these differences. I have to look beyond whether an object seems out of place and consider who uses it, what its removal would change, and how much of the place needs to be brought under our control.

I find it easier to recognise that demand in software because responsibility there belongs to my daily work. Outdoors, I am learning to distrust the ease with which I can approve a cleared space. The word clean arrives with so much approval already attached that I can use it before attending to what has gone. I want to hold the judgment open long enough for the less visible consequences to enter it.

At work I will go on removing things whose avoidable costs other people have had to carry. In the city, I want to leave more room for lives that can continue without becoming part of our plan. I may have to walk around a branch, accept a thicker patch of bushes, let a place remain harder to inspect. Some of what I would have called unfinished can stay that way while something else grows there.

Sources

  1. European Environment Agency: soil sealing. Explains how impermeable surfaces cover soil and put its biological functions at risk. The opening observations are my own; this source supports their ecological context.
  2. Max Ferlauto: leaf litter management, doctoral dissertation (2024). The abstract reports effects on overwintering insects, spring emergence and the soil microclimate in residential landscapes. These findings support the passage about consequences that become visible in another season; they do not establish the same outcome for every site or species.
  3. Dawson and colleagues: wood-inhabiting fungal communities (2024). The deposited data and study abstract describe associations between decay stages and fungal communities in boreal spruce wood. This supports the changing conditions within deadwood, without making every fallen tree follow one fixed succession.
  4. IUCN: peatlands and climate change. Explains the role of waterlogging and slow decomposition in peat accumulation, and the consequences of drainage. Peatland hydrology differs from the process inside a rotting trunk.
  5. GitHub: archiving repositories. Documents a specific way to end active maintenance while leaving a repository read-only, searchable and available for forking. Archiving does not promise permanent availability or eliminate all costs.
  6. Software Carbon Intensity specification. Provides the accounting context for operational energy and embodied hardware emissions. It supports including physical equipment in the discussion, without converting deleted bytes directly into energy savings or accounting for every ecological consequence.
  7. Milias-Argeitis and colleagues: automated optogenetic feedback control (2016). Demonstrates computer-controlled light regulating gene expression and growth in E. coli cultures. The arrangement can keep affecting organisms without a fresh human action for each adjustment; the cells remain dependent on their medium and apparatus.
  8. Haram and colleagues: life on floating plastic (2023). The deposited data and abstract document coastal invertebrates living and reproducing on North Pacific plastic. Their presence complicates removal decisions and raises dispersal concerns; it does not demonstrate an overall benefit from plastic pollution.
  9. City of Helsinki: forest-management approach (2026). Provides practical context for shared urban space. The city describes retaining deadwood and allowing natural ageing alongside safe recreation. These are management intentions; their actual results require separate assessment.
  10. Garbage Collection Is a Professional Skill. My earlier essay considers the obligations created when published work asks other people to support it. Here that responsibility meets the different consequences of clearing an inhabited place.
  11. Existence. An earlier treatment of partial contributions and the pressure to continue them. It informs the distinction between preserving work and requiring its author to keep working.
  12. Slop Is a Lazy Word. Returns here through the concern that an approving or dismissive word can finish a judgment before we have attended to the object.
  13. IPBES: global assessment, summary for policymakers (2019). Section A1 describes human dependence on ecological processes that sustain food, air, fresh water and soils. This supports the biological conditions of our work. The account of the digital as a social creation, and the priority I give to the living world, are my interpretation.
