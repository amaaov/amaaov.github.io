# A Cat on the Desktop

Draft: 13 November 2026  
Author: amaaov  
Language: English

---

There is room on this desktop for a small, unnecessary image of a cat. The picture crosses the page, sometimes moving a letter out of place. A few taps can put the letter back, and a touch can leave the figure in a sleeping pose. I can spend a minute with this modest distraction and then leave it. The minute can be enough in itself.

For more than two decades, making and releasing work has been part of my life. The scale and the means have varied, with blogs and open-source software becoming more prominent in recent years. I have generally worked from my own material, taking relatively little directly from others. Through much of this I have wanted to avoid recognition and identification. I want something I made to be available without having to accompany it everywhere as a recognisable person.

I want the work to be found while keeping some distance between its availability and my own. Someone can encounter a thing I released without needing an account of the person who made it. I would like to leave that encounter possible and return to other things. An account, a profile and a stream of updates can make the work easier to find, but keeping them active can ask me to go on appearing beside it. The wish to publish becomes entangled with a commitment to remain present.

At the same time, the computer lets me do more of the making myself. Operations that once needed separate tools, different kinds of competence, or another person's time can become available in the same working environment. With generative systems, I can begin examining a possible result earlier. An uncertain intention takes a form I can inspect and change before deciding how far to pursue it. I understand the pleasure in having more possibilities than the afternoon can accommodate.

Once another version is easy to obtain, stopping can feel like leaving a useful possibility unexplored. I can use the time I have saved to make another attempt, then another. Finishing something independently can also leave me with the work around its release: presenting it, keeping it visible, reaching people and responding to them. The computer has made more of this manageable, while I still have to decide how much of it I want to take on.

I have tried giving software more of the work of publication and reaching an audience. Its reliability was striking. What unsettled me was the effect of a surrogate in which I could find very little of myself. I could arrange for something to be published and still feel distant from what happened as it spread. Starting the process was easier than grasping how far its effects might reach.

That distance makes it harder for me to say what I am responsible for as an author. Choosing a purpose, accepting a result and allowing it to circulate still have consequences, even when I can scarcely recognise myself in the resulting words or image. I can understand the procedure and be surprised by what follows. A short instruction draws on other people's decisions, stored material, prepared machinery and a great deal of earlier work. What I set in motion extends well beyond the part I have made myself.

Software can also prepare material at the recipient's request. A person asks for an explanation or a selection suited to the time they have, then changes the result through a preference or a correction. Receiving becomes part of making. Many operations may occur before that person sees the result, but their number tells us little by itself about how many people were reached or how much attention they gave.

I find it useful to keep those quantities separate. A program may examine many files for one person who still has only one evening to spend with the result. More processing can require more computing resources while that person's available time remains the same. An exchange can also be sufficient on a much smaller scale: one person needs something, another makes it, and both find what they came for. I want to keep room for that possibility when thinking about a growing audience.

Reaching someone depends partly on how people are able to find the work. Recommendations, search, subscriptions and direct links offer different paths through what is available. The people who design and maintain these systems choose which information to use and what to favour. I may be free to publish while having little influence over the conditions under which someone will find what I made.

Computerism is the name I want to try for the authority exercised through these arrangements. People and organisations set the purposes for computations that help determine which work appears, which response counts and which opportunities become available. I encounter the result as the ordinary order of a screen. It can be difficult to see whose choices produced that order or where I could question them. The power extends further when participation requires using the routes those people control.

A route can change while a person is still using it. A music-generation service allowed people to continue creating while withdrawing the ability to download their work. Making something inside the service and taking the result elsewhere had become separate permissions. The studio I can operate by myself may still have doors controlled by somebody else.

I want to keep that dependence visible alongside the increased capacity. More people may be able to attempt work they could previously only imagine. Some will make things for themselves, some for friends, some for strangers, some for a living. A larger supply may bring unexpected discoveries as well as many similar results. Its value will depend on what people find and do with the work, which a count of completed objects cannot settle in advance.

What I hope for from a release can be modest. A useful program may need only a few users, and a piece of sound may matter on one occasion. Publishing can give a work a chance to be found after my attention has moved elsewhere. Continuing to make things can be worthwhile while the eventual audience remains uncertain. I would rather keep that uncertainty than turn persistence into a promise of recognition.

The prospect of recognition can draw me back into keeping myself visible. An archive can preserve access to a work, while a personal channel may require its maker to keep appearing. I would like to choose those commitments separately. A person should be able to finish, preserve, hand over or stop, with care for those who already depend on what was made. I want the freedom to make more to include a way of bringing an obligation to an end.

Deciding how long to remain involved also matters within the making itself. With sound, I can want a result and the experience of sounding. Holding a tone, joining another person or allowing a silence to continue can be part of what I came for. The time spent matters to the experience. A quicker route to a similar audible result may serve another purpose very well while leaving this desire unfulfilled.

I use musicioning as a name for participation whose meaning can exceed the sound it leaves behind. For me, it can approach religious experience. I can seek it by myself, although company can make it easier to reach. I want to choose the means for the occasion, with a computer among the possibilities. The experience and the making of an audible result each deserve attention.

When I speak of organic expression, I am thinking of the direct bodily act of sounding, drawing or shaping material. Computer expression proceeds through encoded representations that a device processes and displays. Both ways of making involve living people and physical instruments. The hardware's material existence does not make its representations organic. A recording can carry a trace of a performance to another listener; the experience of performing belongs to those who took part. I can work with recordings and still want another relation to material, duration and other people.

Being able to produce more leaves these desires open. A machine's capabilities may change the demand for a skill, the price of a result or the standing of a profession. What a practice means to me also comes from the time I have spent in it. I can care about the result and about what happens while making it, and need different ways to judge them. As more means become available, more people may discover reasons of their own to continue.

The figure returns to the edge of the writing. On the screen, images and changes of pose sustain an illusion of a cat. I can follow the apparent movement and give the figure my attention, while the animal it depicts is absent. Touching the picture changes what the program displays. I recognise the pose of a sleeping cat; watching the figure is something I do in my own life.

I like being able to use a computer for an occasion as small as this. On the same machine I can work with sound, prepare a release or spend time with a moving image that crosses the desktop again. I have different reasons for each, and some can remain a small part of an ordinary day. I can enjoy them without taking on a career, seeking recognition or undertaking to remain available.

As the means of making multiply, choosing how far to follow each possibility becomes harder. I want to release work and choose how much of myself to commit to it. I need to notice when I am continuing simply because the procedure makes it easy. I also want to remain open to another person's intention, including one that changes what I was going to do next. Making more can leave more things for someone to find, while such a meeting still depends on what happens between us.

For now there is a document, a moving illustration and a few small repairs to make. You can leave the page with a letter still out of place. The work can remain available, and I can turn to something else without knowing whether anyone will return to it.
